# Training Policy Framework

**Polarized Periodized Rowing Plan**  
**Milestone:** R9F.5a.2 | **Version:** 1.0 | **Date:** November 26, 2026  
**Status:** Foundational policy approved; operational parameters and implementation pending

## 1. Purpose and authority

This framework consolidates the 55 conceptual decisions approved through six rounds of design discussion. It governs the design of an individualized, evidence-informed rowing planner for athletes and coaches, with particular attention to masters rowing. It is not a clinical protocol, a validated physiological model, or a claim that all capabilities already exist in the application.

The planner shall support development, maintenance, competition preparation, recovery, and adaptation to real-world circumstances. It shall remain useful without detailed workout logs or wearable devices. Recommendations shall reflect the strength and applicability of evidence, athlete goals, preferences, constraints, and actual training response.

### Policy interpretation

- **Shall** denotes an approved design requirement; **should** denotes a recommended default or objective that permits justified exceptions; **may** denotes an authorized option.
- Conceptual decisions are approved. Numerical thresholds, exact progression increments, loading-block durations, recovery intervals, category allocation ranges, and implementation details require separate review and approval.
- Illustrative numerical schedules from the design discussions are not approved prescription rules.
- Existing hard constraints, explicit health restrictions, and authoritative commitments remain protected. Conflicts shall be surfaced rather than silently resolved by violating constraints. Existing safeguards remain in effect until a reviewed replacement is approved and validated.
- Athlete autonomy, permission to use readiness information, and permission to use particular modalities or demanding session combinations are separate controls.
- Research shall prioritize direct rowing and masters-athlete evidence, including female masters athletes where available; extrapolations and configurable coaching judgments shall be identified explicitly.

## 2. Starting training dose (Policies 1–7)

### 1. Hybrid starting-dose model
Use established training workload, general and rowing-specific experience, goals, and available capacity together. Available time is a ceiling, not a target. An experienced endurance athlete new to rowing is not necessarily a sedentary beginner, but rowing-specific tolerance must be established separately. Recommend a provisional starting dose when evidence is limited.

### 2. Category- and modality-specific baselines
Track overall physical duration, rowing duration and frequency, ergometer and on-water exposure, strength frequency and duration, other aerobic modalities, quality exposures, and typical recovery patterns where evidence is available. Additional detail is optional. A category-specific increase can represent a change in demand even when overall minutes remain unchanged. An isolated deviation from the prescribed category mix ordinarily does not change the broader plan; repeated deviations over a rolling 7–14-day review window warrant evaluation. Immediate sequencing implications, restrictions, or significant safety concerns may require earlier action. Planned category progression itself must be justified rather than waiting for deviations to occur.

### 3. Multiple observation windows
Use multiple windows for different purposes: recent exposure and sequencing, accumulated demand, current baseline, and longer-term training consistency. The agreed approach includes 7- and 14-day reviews, with approximately 28 days and 8–12 weeks as proposed baseline and longer-term observation horizons. These are descriptive review windows, not physiological recovery thresholds or mandatory minimum data requirements. Preserve continuity across weeks and plan regeneration.

### 4. Incomplete and conflicting history
Use reliable completed records when sufficiently representative; accept credible self-report as provisional evidence when records are sparse. Ask athletes to clarify material discrepancies instead of arbitrarily averaging conflicting values. With neither source, use a provisional experience-informed recommendation. Refine the baseline as reliable evidence becomes available. Missing records do not establish inactivity or rest; reported aggregate data must not be converted into invented dated workouts.

### 5. Workload and tolerance are distinct
Distinguish what an athlete has undertaken from how well they have tolerated it. Invite simple athlete reports of consistency, challenge, and recovery, supplemented by actual completion and comparable performance evidence when available. Represent uncertainty explicitly. Do not diagnose overtraining or infer confirmed tolerance from volume alone.

### 6. Goals guide composition before volume
Goals primarily influence developmental emphasis, workout purpose, and category composition. Increase overall or category-specific training minutes progressively only when the additional stimulus is justified and tolerance evidence supports it. Improved performance does not necessarily require more minutes.

### 7. Age, sex, and life stage
Use age, sex, and voluntarily disclosed life-stage considerations as context, informed by applicable research. Do not automatically apply universal age- or sex-based volume reductions or recovery extensions. Individualize using experience, history, capability, and response. Identify gaps in evidence concerning female masters athletes rather than inventing demographic coefficients.

## 3. Training progression (Policies 8–14)

### 8. Objective-specific progression
Progress the training variables most relevant to the current developmental objective: duration, frequency, quality work, interval structure, target intensity, strength demand, technical complexity, or modality-specific exposure. Avoid simultaneous substantial increases in multiple demanding variables without adequate justification. Strength progression at this stage concerns purpose and general demand, not individualized resistance prescriptions.

### 9. Hybrid planned and response-dependent progression
Provide a forward-looking periodized plan containing provisional progression opportunities. Evaluate actual response before confirming increases. Do not automatically advance solely because calendar time elapsed, and do not require exhaustive workout logs to consider progression. Simple completion checkboxes and periodic athlete confirmation are legitimate evidence sources, with their limitations preserved.

### 10. Multiple evidence pathways for tolerance
Support detailed tracking (actual workload and performance, HR, RPE, and feedback), basic tracking (completion and difficulty), and minimal tracking (periodic athlete confirmation of consistency and tolerance). No single measure guarantees tolerance. Missing feedback does not prove readiness. Report evidence limitations when recommending progression.

### 11. Regular progression review
Review training at regular intervals regardless of developmental emphasis. Use daily review for immediate sequencing, weekly review for progression opportunities, 7–14-day patterns for meaningful deviations, and broader reviews around four weeks or block boundaries. Review does not mean increase; assess whether training remains appropriately challenging. Exact review implementation may be refined separately.

### 12. Context-dependent progression guardrails
Use research-informed absolute and relative limits differentiated by training category, modality, intensity, experience, and tolerance. Do not assume a universal weekly percentage rule. Numerical guardrails require evidence review and approval; where evidence is insufficient, label them configurable coaching policy.

### 13. Plateau and interruption interpretation
Investigate training consistency, illness or other interruption, recovery, stimulus suitability, assessment comparability, technique, and time for adaptation before responding to a plateau. Depending on evidence, maintain, increase, change, or reduce stimulus. Do not automatically increase volume or intensity after stagnant performance.

### 14. Athlete and coach overrides
Allow ordinary progression, volume, emphasis, and distribution recommendations to be adjusted by the athlete or authorized coach. Explain departures from the recommendation and preserve applicable hard constraints and existing override semantics. The application shall not override medical restrictions or issue medical clearance.

## 4. Recovery and consolidation (Policies 15–23)

### 15. Proactive and response-dependent recovery
Anticipate recovery opportunities as part of the plan, while adapting their timing and magnitude to evidence of response. Do not require excessive fatigue before incorporating reduced-demand training.

### 16. Individualized loading-to-recovery patterns
Recommend loading and lighter-period structures based on research where available, experience, developmental emphasis, competition schedule, recent exposure, and individual response. Permit athlete or coach adjustment. Do not assume any fixed loading-to-recovery ratio is universally optimal or assign shorter cycles solely because of age. Exact cycle lengths remain pending scientific review.

### 17. Distinct consolidation and recovery periods
Distinguish consolidation (reinforcing adaptation while maintaining or reducing selected demands) from recovery (more deliberate reduction of demand following competition, interruption, or accumulated difficulty). Adjust the dimensions most relevant to preceding stimulus: endurance duration, interval work, strength demand, neuromuscular exposure, or frequency. Maintenance is also distinct from developmental loading.

### 18. Deliberate daily variation
Seek meaningful variation in daily demand rather than uniformly distributing minutes across available days. Consider duration, intensity, muscular and neuromuscular loading, purpose, combined sessions, and practical constraints. Do not assume short or easy sessions are necessarily recovery-purpose sessions, or impose identical hard/easy patterns on all athletes.

### 19. Complete-rest authority
Support fixed complete rest as a hard scheduling constraint; flexible requested complete rest as a strong preference whose unresolved conflicts are explicitly reported; and plan-managed recovery, which may use reduced-demand exercise, technical work, or complete rest. Do not silently reinterpret existing flexible-rest preferences as authorization for daily training. Do not impose a universal minimum number of complete-rest days absent an applicable constraint or justified recommendation.

### 20. Multidimensional recovery
Consider cardiovascular/metabolic, muscular, neuromuscular, accumulated overall, and where relevant technical/cognitive demand. These dimensions interact and may be incompletely observed. Known strength duration does not establish muscular load; zero known endurance-quality work does not mean zero physiological demand. Do not invent a composite fatigue or recovery score.

### 21. Same-volume consolidation and reduced-volume recovery
Permit consolidation at similar physical duration when relevant demands are meaningfully reduced. Permit genuine reductions in total physical duration when appropriate. Do not automatically replace every removed demanding minute with additional easy training merely to conserve the original target. Conservation shall apply to the final approved dose, with any adjustment explicitly reconciled upstream.

### 22. No automatic training debt
Ordinarily drop missed or deliberately removed training rather than redistribute it across remaining days. Consider rescheduling key developmental sessions or assessments when their purpose, timing, feasibility, and recovery context justify it. Do not cram missed training into a return period.

### 23. Hybrid recovery completion
Use planned duration and available response evidence to guide return to loading. Ordinary consolidation may transition according to the planned schedule absent contrary information, without claiming confirmed recovery. After significant interruption, illness, or unusual fatigue, use a more cautious progressive return and applicable restrictions. Missing feedback is not evidence of recovery.

## 5. Combined training (Policies 24–33)

### 24. Strength training by default, with opt-out
Recommend strength training as appropriate to goals, experience, equipment, phase, and tolerance; allow athletes to decline. Respect access, ability, and restrictions, including externally provided strength programs.

### 25. Seasonal strength emphasis
Vary strength frequency, duration, and purpose in connection with seasonal and developmental goals. Distinguish athlete-preferred frequency from authoritative commitments. Do not prescribe a fixed year-round strength pattern irrespective of phase.

### 26. Strength prescription scope
Initially provide duration, strength type, developmental purpose, and illustrative movement categories or exercise examples. Possible purposes include foundational strength, maximal strength, hypertrophy, explosive strength, strength endurance, maintenance, and reduced-demand work. Do not attempt comprehensive individualized lifting programs, resistance targets, or detailed load progression without sufficient information and a separately approved capability. Accept externally prescribed strength sessions.

### 27. Combined versus separate training
Base consolidation of workouts on daily availability and the athlete's preference to combine or separate sessions, including morning/evening doubles and day-specific preferences. If the athlete has no preference, consider developmental and recovery objectives. Do not combine workouts solely to fit a minute target or override an explicit preference silently.

### 28. Demanding double-session permission
The athlete determines through their profile whether demanding same-day combinations are permitted. Permission is necessary but does not establish physiological suitability. Within that permission, evaluate primary objective, session priority, experience, recent demand, separation, subsequent schedule, and applicable constraints. Existing spacing safeguards remain until reviewed and replaced.

### 29. Workout order
When sessions are flexible, prioritize the workout most important to the developmental objective. Respect coached practices, availability, and athlete preferences. Recommend separation where appropriate; do not impose an unsupported universal separation interval. Distinguish recommendations from mandatory constraints.

### 30. Seasonal cross-training and substitutions
Support supplemental aerobic work, standalone cross-training, and temporary substitutions as separate authorizations. Allow seasonal preferences: for example, standalone cross-training may provide winter ergometer variety while on-water seasons may favor rowing and use alternatives mainly when weather or water conditions prevent rowing. Cross-training is a legitimate developmental tool, not merely a feasibility workaround. Protect justified rowing-specific exposure and acknowledge that modalities are not universally interchangeable.

### 31. Seasonal rowing specificity
Generally increase rowing specificity as important rowing competitions approach when appropriate; allow more modality diversity during general preparation and transition. Adjust for goals, equipment, season, experience, and current modality-specific tolerance. No universal allocation percentages are approved.

### 32. Developmental roles and priorities
Assign contextual roles to workouts: primary developmental, supporting developmental, maintenance, technical, recovery, or optional supplemental. Use these roles when modifying a schedule or dose instead of reducing every workout proportionally. Recovery, restrictions, and authoritative commitments can take precedence over key developmental stimuli. A role does not imply an inflexible ranking.

### 33. Dimension-specific experience
Evaluate experience separately across rowing technique and exposure, general endurance, strength, high-intensity work, combined/double sessions, and competition. An athlete may be advanced in one area and new to another. Keep profile entry simple and optional where detail is unavailable; do not use an artificial universal athlete score.

## 6. Special circumstances (Policies 34–44)

### 34. Competition priority versus physiological demand
Retain A/B/C priority as strategic context for preparation, taper, and disruption to development. Separately characterize actual or expected race demand. A lower-priority maximal race may impose substantial demand. Do not assume strategic priority alone determines physiological recovery.

### 35. Individualized taper
Use research-supported taper strategies differentiated by race distance and type, number of events, preceding load, phase, priority, and athlete experience. Consider duration, demanding work, target intensity, muscular demand, and frequency separately. Numerical taper parameters require further review.

### 36. Individual races within competition episodes
Record individual race events, including distance, date, boat class, priority and expected/actual effort or performance where known. Group events within a regatta or multi-day competition episode to characterize cumulative exposure, including warm-ups and cool-downs when recorded. Allow provisional event information and update as entries become known. Unknown duration is not zero work.

### 37. Competition recovery
Use research-informed initial guidance and adjust to competition demand, recent exposures, number and length of events, athlete response, and upcoming objectives. Include travel or other contextual demands when reported. Existing A/B/C hard and soft recovery rules are interim safeguards, not proven physiological durations; review before replacing them.

### 38. Connected competition periods
Plan closely spaced competitions as a connected period, particularly during frequent fall head races. Protect principal performance objectives while integrating secondary races into development when appropriate. A race may replace compatible quality training rather than be added to an already demanding plan. Do not mechanically apply independent full taper/recovery cycles to every event.

### 39. Distinguish training interruption causes
Allow simple reporting of work/family commitments, travel, vacation, weather, equipment limitations, illness, injury, unusual fatigue, or other disruptions. Consider duration and actual activity; a hiking vacation differs from inactivity due to illness. Determine whether immediate scheduling, progression, or the broader developmental block warrants change.

### 40. Interrupted developmental blocks
Evaluate actual completed developmental exposure, interruption type, recovery, seasonal timing, and upcoming race deadlines before extending, repeating, shortening, or changing a block. Neither elapsed calendar time nor a day-for-day extension establishes adequate developmental exposure.

### 41. Return after illness or injury
Support athlete-initiated interruption and progressive-return states, including not ready, restricted, gradual return, and already resumed. Respect explicit restrictions and appropriate professional guidance; suspend inappropriate progression. Do not diagnose, provide medical clearance, or infer readiness from elapsed time or medication completion. Health-related constraints take precedence over developmental goals and automation preferences.

### 42. Temporary versus permanent availability
Support temporary availability exceptions and ongoing profile changes separately. Protect key stimuli where practical, without forcing the original dose into inadequate capacity. Increased availability alone does not justify increased workload; evidence of tolerance and developmental need remains necessary.

### 43. Individual workout substitution
Allow athletes to replace or modify individual workouts without unnecessary whole-plan regeneration. Consider reason, authorized modalities, availability, developmental purpose, demand, and immediate sequencing. Prefer rowing-specific equivalents when appropriate; disclose when an alternative only preserves general aerobic purpose. Retain original and revised prescriptions and record actual completion separately. Repeated substitutions inform the 7–14-day review.

### 44. Meaningful unplanned activity
Allow recording of meaningful unplanned exercise or substantial recreational activity and consider its actual demand in subsequent decisions. Provide optional activity-specific details such as duration, distance, HR, speed/pace, perceived effort, rowing watts/split/stroke rate, conditions, or strength purpose. Ordinary daily activities need not be logged. Do not automatically increase future recommended dose because an athlete did extra exercise.

## 7. Adaptive planning and athlete feedback (Policies 45–55)

### 45. Athlete-selectable planning autonomy
Support four profile-selectable levels: advisory (identify changes, athlete acts), assisted (propose changes for approval), adaptive (default; authorized minor adjustments automatically, substantial changes require approval), and fully adaptive (automatic changes within explicitly authorized boundaries). Selecting a level does not authorize private readiness-data use or bypass hard constraints, commitments, or health restrictions. Existing users' authorization must not be silently broadened.

### 46. Minor versus substantial changes
Classify adjustments by consequences, not only minutes. Examples of potentially minor changes include rescheduling flexible ordinary workouts, authorized substitutions, and limited workout structure or duration adjustments preserving purpose. Changes to sustained dose, frequency, developmental emphasis, block structure, or important competition preparation are ordinarily substantial. Respect autonomy settings, seek approval where required, and explain what changed and why. Exact classification rules and authorization limits remain pending operational approval.

### 47. Low-burden feedback and regular reviews
Make workout completion simple; detailed logging remains optional. Encourage short weekly check-ins covering consistency, overall challenge, recovery, and disruptions. Include broader developmental reviews and event-triggered check-ins after competition, interruption, or meaningful changes. Avoid repetitive daily questionnaires. Distinguish reported completion from measured fulfillment of every prescribed interval.

### 48. Separate readiness-data authorization
Support private monitoring (no prescription use), recommendation support (approval required), and authorized adaptation (selected automatic changes). Obtain explicit permission before using sensitive readiness information for automatic changes. Potential inputs include sleep, fatigue, soreness, stress, resting HR, HRV, motivation, and athlete-reported restrictions. No single measure establishes ability to train. Explicit training restrictions are separate from private monitoring data and must be respected.

### 49. Subjective difficulty and objective execution
Collect perceived difficulty relative to intended workout demand, optional conventional RPE, and relevant objective data. For HR-guided endurance work, prioritize time within/above/below the individualized target band when reliable time-series data are available; average and peak HR provide additional context. Interpret warm-up, work, recovery, and cool-down separately where possible. Consider HR drift, measurement quality, environmental conditions, modality, power, split, stroke rate, and comparable conditions. Do not infer time in band from average or peak HR alone. For short high-power efforts, use relevant power and interval measures rather than treating HR as the sole target. Missing measurements remain unknown.

### 50. Target mismatch and athlete education
Distinguish inappropriate targets from excessive overall training demand, execution differences, environmental conditions, technique, illness, or measurement error. Explain how to approach subsequent workouts using the athlete's recorded data. For example, a difficult UT2 session with sustained HR above target warrants reviewing execution and context; it does not automatically prove poor fitness or require reducing the entire plan. Evaluate repeated comparable patterns before recalibrating targets or changing developmental dose.

### 51. Decision-relevant and scheduled assessments
Recommend targeted assessments when new evidence would meaningfully improve decisions; avoid routinely repeating an entire battery without purpose. Allow athletes and coaches to schedule individual tests or multi-day team testing batteries in the profile early in the season or closer to the event. Respect authoritative dates, account for preparation and recovery, replace compatible demanding exposures where appropriate, and preserve historical results. Distinguish maximal and submaximal protocols, modality, and comparability. Allow accept/defer/decline for planner recommendations; do not imply medical readiness for maximal tests.

### 52. Transparent evidence quality
Communicate whether recommendations are provisional, individualized, or longitudinally informed based on relevant reliable evidence, not athlete ability or invented numerical confidence scores. Explain that logging and use of the application can improve individualization, without requiring wearables or detailed entries. More data does not guarantee better recommendations if it is unreliable or incomparable.

### 53. Expandable explanations
Provide concise default explanations of workout purpose, placement, duration, intended effort, and reasons for changes. Offer expandable detail explaining relevant evidence, constraints, uncertainty, and rationale. Include educational feedback on execution and identify provisional coaching choices rather than presenting them as established science.

### 54. Preserve plan and actual history
Retain original prescription, subsequent authorized modifications, and actual completed activity with distinct identities and provenance. Keep the calendar uncluttered while allowing review of change history. Evaluate completion against the relevant revised prescription while preserving the original for longitudinal analysis. Avoid double-counting the same exposure or treating planned work as actual work.

### 55. Athlete disagreement and overrides
Allow accept, defer, modify, maintain current plan, or consult a coach as appropriate. Explain implications, preserve restrictions and authority limits, record the decision, and consider it in future planning. Do not repeatedly present a declined recommendation absent new evidence or a meaningful contextual change.

## 8. Cross-policy planning behavior

The following synthesis organizes the approved decisions; it does not establish new numerical thresholds or a final software algorithm.

### Decision layers

| Layer | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Context and authority | Goals, profile, availability, permissions, fixed commitments, restrictions |
| Evidence | Completed training, reported baselines, assessments, prospective commitments, uncertainty |
| Development | Primary and supporting adaptations, maintenance, seasonal and race priorities |
| Sustainable dose | Distinguish available capacity, established workload, tolerance, recommended stimulus, and recovery-adjusted dose |
| Distribution | Select appropriate workout roles, modalities, duration, intensity, strength purpose, and recovery opportunities |
| Feasibility and reconciliation | Explore authorized alternatives, preserve constraints, revise upstream dose when warranted, validate final prescription |
| Feedback and adaptation | Record completion and modifications, interpret response, propose or apply authorized changes |

Three adaptation horizons apply: immediate session/sequence adjustment; repeated-pattern review (7–14 days); and broader developmental/block review. A single deviation generally does not justify a new block or dose. Restrictions and urgent scheduling/sequence conflicts need not wait for a pattern review.

### Evidence integrity

Maintain separate actual, authoritative future, and generated future exposures. Preserve unknown duration and characteristics as unknown. Distinguish descriptive rolling minutes from physiological response; do not use an unsupported aggregate stress, fatigue, or acute-to-chronic workload ratio as a prescription trigger. Use modality-specific and protocol-comparable performance observations. Preserve race and assessment occurrences even when physical duration is unknown. Separate strength from endurance-intensity accounting.

### Conflict handling

When scheduling fails, first distinguish insufficient capacity or conflicting preferences from physiological intolerance. Explore valid arrangements and authorized alternatives without violating protected constraints. Where recovery or feasibility justifies changing dose, explicitly reconcile the new recommendation and final allocation; do not silently discard minutes or force their conservation at the expense of recovery. Explain unresolved conflicts and seek athlete or coach input when required.
