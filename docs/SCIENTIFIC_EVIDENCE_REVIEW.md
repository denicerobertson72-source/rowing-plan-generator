# Scientific Evidence Review

Polarized Periodized Rowing Plan | R9F.5a.4a | v1.0 | 2026-11-26

**Status:** Consolidated targeted review, core references checked; supplementary citation audit remains open. Companion to `TRAINING_POLICY_FRAMEWORK.md` and `OPERATIONAL_TRAINING_POLICIES.md`. This is not a systematic review, clinical guideline or validation of the software.

## Method and interpretation

The ten research packages consolidate the earlier design discussions. In this pass, targeted searches and PubMed/publisher records were checked for the core sources below. Verification means title and relevant abstract or accessible text were inspected, not independent replication, full-text critical appraisal or formal risk-of-bias grading. Sources that could not be checked in this pass are retained in a separate verification queue, not used as confirmed evidence. Direct masters rowing intervention evidence is sparse; distinguish rowing-specific findings, other masters populations, other endurance athletes and general populations. Study design alone does not guarantee applicability. Numerical policy choices are approved for pilot, not established optimal or safe thresholds.

## S1 — Dose and progression (OP-01,06)

- **E01:** Zhong et al. (2025), elite rowing systematic review, nine studies/82 rowers. Weekly volumes varied widely (10–31 hours, typically 14–20); seasonal intensity distribution varied. No model clearly superior. Elite volumes are not targets for masters athletes. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40185480/), DOI 10.1123/ijspp.2024-0433.
- **E02:** Damsted et al. (2018), systematic review of running workload and injury. Evidence did not establish the common 10% rule or a universal hazardous increase threshold. Indirect sport applicability; does not prove that any particular rowing increase is safe. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6253751/).
- **E03:** Soligard et al. (2016), IOC consensus on load and injury risk. Supports considering external exposure and individual response in context, rather than prescribing by minutes alone. Consensus, not a validated rowing risk predictor. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27535989/).
- **E04:** Saw et al. (2016), systematic review of subjective and objective athlete monitoring. Subjective well-being is useful in monitoring response, not a guarantee of readiness. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26423706/).

**Application:** distinguish capacity, established workload, tolerance, developmental dose, recovery-adjusted dose and feasible prescription. Independent category and modality review prevents a 500-minute plan with 240 rowing minutes becoming 500 minutes with 335 rowing minutes (+39.6%) without justification. Basic completion and periodic confirmation can inform progression. The 5–10-minute easy-session increment, 2–5% weekly progression and 7/14/28-day and 8–12-week review horizons are coaching/software policy, not experimentally verified cutoffs. No automatic compounding or acute:chronic workload-ratio risk score.

## S2 — Masters physiology and recovery (OP-02,03,06)

- **E05:** Borges, Reaburn, Driller & Argus (2016), narrative review of masters performance and recovery kinetics. Limited recovery research; possible age effects, with activity and conditioning relevant. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25880787/), DOI 10.1123/japa.2015-0021.
- **E06:** Age- and Sex-Related Differences in Recovery From High-Intensity and Endurance Exercise (2021), brief review. Age/sex differences depend on recovery outcome and exercise context; no universal masters correction follows. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33883293/).
- **E07:** Vangsgaard et al. (2026), scoping review of 29 studies of women masters endurance athletes. Age-related aerobic changes are documented; menopause effects remain uncertain and economy/lactate evidence mixed. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42072980/), DOI 10.3390/healthcare14081080.
- **E08:** Li et al. (2024), umbrella review of 22 reviews, 63 studies and 1,100 endurance athletes. No recovery intervention consistently benefited athletes across the evidence; this does not directly compare optimal rest-day schedules. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38753045/), DOI 10.1186/s40798-024-00724-6.

**Application:** evaluate cardiovascular/metabolic, muscular, neuromuscular, overall and technical demand separately. Age, sex and voluntary life-stage details are contextual, not automatic dose or recovery multipliers. Private readiness stays outside automated planning without explicit authorization. Active recovery, technical work and complete rest are options; no universal rest-day minimum established. Missing history is not evidence of inactivity or recovery.

## S3 — Periodization and consolidation (OP-02–05)

- **E09:** Mølmen et al. (2019), systematic review/meta-analysis of block versus traditional endurance periodization. Small favorable effects for selected aerobic outcomes; small, heterogeneous studies limit inference. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6802561/).
- **E10:** Spiering et al. (2021), narrative review of minimal maintenance dose. General populations can sometimes maintain endurance and strength with substantial volume/frequency reductions while retaining appropriate intensity; insufficient athlete-specific data for exact prescriptions. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/).
- **E01** also supports seasonal flexibility rather than a universal periodization model.

**Application:** hybrid developmental blocks with primary/supporting/maintenance purposes; separate block duration from loading cycles. 2:1, 3:1, 4:1 templates; 0–10%, 10–25%, 25–40% volume reduction options; 4–12-week developmental ranges by purpose (see OP-05) are pilot conventions, not proven optimums. Consolidation may reduce demanding composition without reducing total minutes; recovery can require more reduction or rest. Return to established tolerated dose before new progression. No calendar-only adaptation claims.

## S4 — Strength and concurrent training (OP-07–09)

- **E11:** Thiele et al. (2020), rowing-specific systematic review/meta-analysis of strength training. Supports inclusion of strength for lower-body force and rowing performance; study limitations and populations restrict precise seasonal dose inference. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32216524/).
- **E12:** Fragala et al. (2019), NSCA position statement on resistance training for older adults. Supports progressive individualized resistance training and attention to strength/power across aging. General older adults, not a masters rowing seasonal trial. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31343601/).
- **E13:** Schumann et al. (2022), systematic review/meta-analysis of concurrent aerobic and strength training. Maximal strength and hypertrophy broadly compatible; explosive strength more susceptible to interference, particularly within the same session. Separation is a scheduling consideration, not proof of full recovery. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/).

**Application:** phase-sensitive strength frequency/duration and purpose-specific examples, not comprehensive resistance prescriptions. 1–3 sessions per week and 20–60-minute context-dependent ranges are pilot envelopes, not direct experimental recommendations for rowing. Easy aerobic combinations permitted within authority; demanding doubles require permission and suitability; at least three hours soft preference where applicable, not a safety minimum. Keep existing hard guards until tested replacements.

## S5 — Specificity and cross-training (OP-10,11,26)

- **E14:** Tanaka (1994), narrative review of aerobic transfer among cycling/running/swimming. Supports partial transfer but sport specificity remains relevant; old, indirect evidence for rowing, not a conversion formula. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7871294/).
- **E15:** Physiological responses to ergometer and on-water incremental rowing tests (2010), seven male rowers, comparative study. Erg/on-water relationships may support provisional transfer, with individual field verification needed. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20861524/).
- **E01:** Seasonal rowing composition is variable, not a validated fixed allocation.

**Application:** separate row-specific and general aerobic exposure; retain winter variety and weather alternatives with distinct permissions for standalone, supplemental and substitution use. Phase allocation percentages in OP-10 are coaching envelopes; no required cross-training for opt-out athletes. No watt-for-watt or minute-for-minute equivalence; crew coordination and blade work are not transferred from generic cardio. Modality-specific targets and histories.

## S6 — Intensity distribution (OP-12–14)

- **E01:** Pyramidal patterns common in elite rowing, with increasing polarization in competition preparation; no clear universally superior model.
- **E16:** Seiler & Kjerland (2006), observational study, 11 male junior cross-country skiers. 17% of endurance sessions high intensity by session goal versus 2.6% of HR observations above VT2; not an intervention or masters study. [Publisher](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.2004.00418.x).
- **E17:** Jamnick et al. (2020), review of exercise-intensity determination. Supports distinction between individualized physiological thresholds and generic fixed-percentage estimates. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32729096/).

**Application:** low-intensity emphasis, phase-sensitive pyramidal/polarized/mixed approaches; no fixed 80/20 mandate. Distinguish physical duration, session-goal count, rowing work minutes, all-endurance intensity minutes, measured HR and power. Strength is excluded from endurance denominator; short PP is characterized separately. Unknown classifications remain visible. Quality frequencies in OP-14 are coaching guidance; count races, demanding coached sessions and assessments. No fixed threshold classification from UT labels alone.

## S7 — Competition, taper and recovery (OP-15–19)

- **E18:** Wang et al. (2023), 14-study endurance taper meta-analysis. Time-trial performance improved; effective strategies included 41–60% reduction with selected intensity/frequency maintained, across taper durations up to 21 days. Not direct masters rowing evidence and not proof that an individual must taper 8–14 days. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37163550/).
- **E01:** Rowing phase variation provides sport-specific context but not a universal taper.

**Application:** A-race 7–14 days (up to 21), 40–60% endurance reduction is a research-informed extrapolation. B 3–7 days/20–40%; C 0–3 days/0–20% are coaching choices. Retain selected race intensity while reducing work volume; no mandatory overload beforehand. Separate priority from actual physiological exposure. Regatta episodes group events without adding duplicate load. Protected recovery periods 0–5 days or individualized by exposure (OP-18), and cluster review at 7/14 days, are provisional policies. No proof of recovery at expiry. Exact race recovery kinetics remain an evidence gap; preserve existing guards until replacement is validated.

## S8 — Interruptions and restoration (OP-20–23)

- **E19:** Zheng et al. (2022), systematic review/meta-analysis, 21 studies of detraining and VO2max in athletes. Both shorter and longer cessation can decrease aerobic capacity; heterogeneity and population differences preclude individual loss estimates from days missed. [Publisher](https://doi.org/10.1155/2022/2130993).
- **E20:** IOC consensus on acute respiratory illness in athletes, part 1 (2022). Supports response-dependent return following respiratory infection, with abnormal symptoms prompting cessation and medical review. Clinical consensus; not an algorithm for diagnosis or clearance, nor a protocol for every injury or illness. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2022-105759).

**Application:** distinguish ordinary inactivity, alternative activity, temporary availability and health restrictions. Restoration ranges 80–100%, 60–80%, 40–60% or individualized by interruption duration (OP-21) are conservative pilot policy, not proven safety percentages. No automatic estimates of fitness lost. Five-state return pathway respects reported restrictions and professional guidance; no elapsed-date clearance. No training debt or compressed catch-up. Travel activity and rowing-specific tolerance may differ.

## S9 — Targets and monitoring (OP-24–28)

- **E21:** Kilbey et al. (2025), systematic review, 24 studies/797 rowers. Lactate-associated power correlates with 2k performance; diverse protocols and small samples constrain comparisons. Association does not establish universal threshold at a given lactate concentration. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40019691/), DOI 10.1186/s40798-024-00796-4.
- **E22:** Clark, McKune & Wood (2025), reliability study of self-regulated 6 km erg training protocol at rate 22; 43 elite rowers including 13 women. Power, HR and ratios showed useful reliability under stable conditions; HR recovery less useful. [Publisher](https://doi.org/10.1177/17479541251345575).
- **E23:** Clark, McKune & Wood (2026), cross-sectional validation of same protocol in 44 male elite rowers. Supports performance/aerobic monitoring but not a standalone recovery assessment. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42335118/).
- **E04, E15, E17:** subjective response, modality specificity and individualized intensity evidence.

**Application:** preserve seven rowing bands with evidence-aware targets; HR/effort for steady endurance, power/pace/repetition quality for appropriate intervals, peak power for short efforts. Established athlete/coach bands not overwritten by new test. Time in band requires suitable trace or reported device summary, not average HR. Power/HR and drift comparisons require comparable conditions; no universal 5% pass/fail, no VO2max or recovery inference from ratio alone. One/two/three mismatch review triggers are pilot software choices; no automatic target adjustment. Optional readiness authorization independent of autonomy.

## S10 — Assessments (OP-29–35)

- **E24:** Borges, Veiga & González-Frutos (2025), systematic review, 34 studies/909 rowers, 72% male. Incremental VO2max and peak power, threshold power and critical power associated with 2k performance. Multiple protocols provide complementary evidence; female masters-specific validity not established. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41283544/), DOI 10.3390/jfmk10040437.
- **E25:** Schabort et al. (1999), repeated 2k tests in eight trained rowers; individual mean-power variability approximately 2%, time variability roughly one-third of that. Small protocol-specific sample; not universal meaningful-change cutoff. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10487463/).
- **E21–23:** threshold assessment and submaximal monitoring support complementary evidence.

**Application:** optional core seven-stroke peak, 60-second maximal, 2k maximal, maximal 30R20; other tests as needed. Core menu is a coaching selection, not a validated composite diagnostic battery. 30R20 measures sustained rate-capped performance, not direct VO2max, LT or CP. Submaximal protocol separate. On-water records retain boat, crew, environmental conditions, speed source and optional instrumentation; no GPS-to-erg power conversion. Review intervals OP-33 are planning opportunities, not expiry dates or compulsory testing. Preserve stable test and episode IDs and history, integrate scheduled tests with recovery and quality exposure. No fixed-ratio diagnosis of limiter.

## Evidence gaps and research governance

No direct evidence in this review validates the full set of 35 numerical and operational rules in masters rowers, particularly women across life stages. Prioritize pilot calibration of category-specific progression, loading templates, consolidation, recovery windows, phase allocations, quality frequency, return doses and test intervals. Track adherence, feedback, unintended changes, reasons for overrides and observed outcomes with consent; do not infer causation from uncontrolled pilot observations. Review sources before making public scientific claims. Record changes by policy ID, source, population, limitation, approval and software version. Full supplementary audit remains a documentation task; it is not permission to silently revise approved pilot parameters.
